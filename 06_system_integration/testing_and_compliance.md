# Testing and Compliance

## Overview

Testing and compliance verification ensure that high-speed serial links meet the performance specifications required for reliable operation in AI systems. This section covers the test instruments, methodologies, and standards used for SerDes characterization.

---

### Q1. What are the primary test instruments used for high-speed serial link characterization?

**Answer:**

Five primary instruments are used for serial link characterization. The vector network analyzer (VNA) measures S-parameters of passive channels in the frequency domain, providing insertion loss, return loss, and crosstalk data from DC to 70+ GHz. Modern VNAs achieve dynamic range of 80-100 dB at 50 GHz, sufficient for characterizing the full channel including connectors and vias.

The bit error rate tester (BERT) generates known data patterns (PRBS7, PRBS13, PRBS31) at the full data rate and counts errors at the receiver. BER testing is the definitive measure of link quality, directly measuring the probability of bit errors. Modern BERTs support up to 112 Gbps PAM4 per channel with integrated pattern generation, error detection, and jitter injection for stressed receiver testing.

The real-time oscilloscope captures the signal waveform at full bandwidth for eye diagram analysis, jitter decomposition, and signal quality measurement. High-performance real-time oscilloscopes achieve 70+ GHz analog bandwidth with 256 GS/s sampling rate, sufficient for 112 GBd signals. The oscilloscope provides the most complete picture of signal quality but is limited by its bandwidth and noise floor.

The sampling oscilloscope (equivalent-time oscilloscope) achieves very high effective bandwidth (80+ GHz) by sampling different points of the waveform across many repetitions. It provides superior signal fidelity for repetitive signals but requires a trigger signal (typically the pattern generator clock) and cannot capture single-shot events.

The time-domain reflectometer (TDR) characterizes the impedance profile of the channel by launching a fast step edge and analyzing the reflections. TDR provides spatial resolution of impedance discontinuities along the channel, identifying vias, connectors, and trace impedance variations. Modern TDR instruments achieve rise times of 15-20 ps, providing approximately 1 mm spatial resolution.

### Q2. What is compliance testing and why is it essential for multi-vendor interoperability?

**Answer:**

Compliance testing verifies that a device's transmitter and receiver meet the specifications defined by the relevant standard (PCIe, Ethernet, USB, etc.) at standardized test points using standardized test procedures. Compliance ensures that any two devices from different vendors that both pass compliance testing will interoperate when connected.

Compliance testing is performed at defined compliance test points (CTPs) in the signal path. For the transmitter, the CTP is typically at the device's output connector or test pad, after the device package but before any external channel. For the receiver, the CTP is at the device's input, with a standardized reference channel applied between the pattern generator and the device.

The compliance test suite for a typical standard like PCIe Gen5 includes transmitter output voltage swing (peak-to-peak differential), transmitter output impedance (return loss at the CTP), transmitter jitter (TJ, RJ, DJ decomposition), transmitter eye diagram (eye height, eye width at BER 1e-6), receiver jitter tolerance (JTOL mask compliance), receiver equalization capability (using reference channels of different loss), and link training and state machine verification.

Compliance testing is typically performed at industry-sponsored compliance workshops (such as the PCI-SIG Compliance Workshop for PCIe) where device vendors bring their hardware and test against reference equipment and other vendors' devices. Passing compliance is required for devices to display the standard's logo and is a prerequisite for sale in most enterprise and server markets.

### Q3. How is stressed receiver testing performed for 112G PAM4 links?

**Answer:**

Stressed receiver testing verifies that the receiver can operate correctly even when the input signal has degraded quality (noise, jitter, ISI). The test simulates worst-case conditions that the receiver might encounter in a deployed system.

The stressed signal is generated by combining a clean PRBS pattern with calibrated impairments: sinusoidal jitter (SJ) at specific frequencies and amplitudes to test jitter tolerance, random jitter (RJ) added by broadband noise generators, ISI from a reference channel (a PCB trace with calibrated insertion loss at Nyquist), and bounded uncorrelated jitter (BUJ) from crosstalk-like interference.

The impairments are calibrated at the receiver's compliance test point using the VNA (for the reference channel S-parameters), the oscilloscope (for the jitter components), and the BERT (for the final BER measurement). The receiver must achieve a BER better than the specified threshold (typically 1e-6 for PAM4 before FEC) with all impairments simultaneously applied.

For PAM4 stressed receiver testing, additional impairments are applied to stress the multi-level signaling: transmitter level non-linearity (RLM degradation), PAM4 timing skew (between MSB and LSB paths), and level-dependent noise. These PAM4-specific impairments exercise the receiver's ability to correctly decide between the four closely-spaced PAM4 levels under adverse conditions.

### Q4. What is TDR measurement and how is it used for channel characterization?

**Answer:**

TDR (Time-Domain Reflectometry) launches a fast-rising voltage step into the channel and records the reflected signal as a function of time. Each impedance discontinuity in the channel creates a reflection: a capacitive discontinuity (impedance decrease) reflects negatively, and an inductive discontinuity (impedance increase) reflects positively. The time of the reflection indicates the physical location of the discontinuity (using the known propagation velocity), and the amplitude indicates the magnitude of the impedance change.

For differential serial links, DTDR (Differential TDR) drives the two conductors with complementary steps and measures the differential reflection, providing the differential impedance profile. The target impedance is typically 100 ohms differential, and the specification usually requires the impedance to remain within plus or minus 10% (90-110 ohms).

TDR is used to identify and diagnose specific impedance discontinuities: BGA pad transitions (appear as negative dips from pad capacitance), via transitions (appear as complex dip-peak patterns from via capacitance and inductance), connector interfaces (appear as distinct impedance signatures characteristic of the connector type), trace impedance variations (appear as gradual impedance trends), and solder joint quality (poor joints create localized impedance anomalies).

### Q5. How is VNA measurement used for S-parameter extraction of serial link channels?

**Answer:**

VNA (Vector Network Analyzer) measurement is the primary method for extracting the frequency-domain characteristics (S-parameters) of serial link channels. The VNA measures the complex (magnitude and phase) reflection and transmission coefficients at each port of the channel across a swept frequency range.

For a differential serial link, a 4-port VNA measurement captures the full mixed-mode S-parameter matrix, including SDD21 (differential insertion loss), SDD11 and SDD22 (differential return loss at each end), SCC21 (common-mode insertion loss), SDC21 and SCD21 (mode conversion), and the cross-coupling terms to adjacent lanes (if a multi-port VNA is used).

The measurement setup requires precision RF connectors (typically 2.92 mm or 1.85 mm connectors for measurements up to 40 GHz or 67 GHz), calibration using standards (SOLT or TRL calibration at the connector planes), and de-embedding of the test fixture (subtracting the fixture's contribution to isolate the DUT's S-parameters).

The VNA data is saved as Touchstone files (.s2p for 2-port, .s4p for 4-port) and used as input to link simulation tools (COM analysis, channel simulation). The data quality must be verified for passivity, causality, and reciprocity before use in simulation, as measurement artifacts can cause inaccurate simulation results.

### Q6. What compliance testing standards apply to AI system interconnects?

**Answer:**

AI systems use multiple interconnect standards, each with its own compliance program. PCIe compliance is managed by PCI-SIG, which defines the electrical specifications for Gen5 (32 GT/s NRZ) and Gen6 (64 GT/s PAM4). The compliance tests cover transmitter output, receiver tolerance, link training, and electrical idle detection. PCI-SIG hosts quarterly compliance workshops where vendors test their devices.

Ethernet compliance for 100G and 400G links is defined by IEEE 802.3 (802.3ck for 100G per lane, 802.3dj for 200G per lane). The compliance tests include transmitter output waveform, receiver sensitivity, FEC performance, and COM analysis of the channel.

OIF (Optical Internetworking Forum) defines the CEI (Common Electrical Interface) specifications for multi-vendor interoperability of 112G and 224G SerDes. CEI-112G defines the electrical interface for chip-to-chip, chip-to-module, and chip-to-backplane links at 112 Gbps per lane.

UCIe compliance is managed by the UCIe Consortium and covers the die-to-die electrical interface (both advanced and standard package modules), the D2D adapter protocol, and the supported upper-layer protocols (PCIe, CXL, streaming).

For NVLink and other proprietary interconnects, there is no multi-vendor compliance program; NVIDIA defines internal specifications and qualification procedures that must be met by the motherboard, connector, and cable vendors that participate in the NVLink ecosystem.

### Q7. How is BER testing performed for PAM4 serial links?

**Answer:**

BER testing for PAM4 serial links uses a BERT configured for 4-level signaling. The transmitter side generates a PAM4 PRBS pattern (such as PRBS13Q or PRBS31Q, which are quaternary versions of the standard PRBS sequences), and the receiver side makes symbol decisions and compares them against the expected pattern.

The BER is measured for each of the three PAM4 eyes independently. The MSB eye BER is determined by errors in the middle threshold (between levels 1 and 2). The LSB eye BER is determined by errors in the upper threshold (between levels 2 and 3) and the lower threshold (between levels 0 and 1). The worst-case eye BER (typically one of the LSB eyes) determines the link's raw BER.

For FEC-enabled links, the raw BER must be below the FEC threshold (approximately 2.4e-4 for RS-544,514). The BERT measures the pre-FEC BER by counting symbol errors over a statistically significant sample (at least 10/BER_target symbols, so approximately 40000 symbols for BER = 2.4e-4 confidence). For deeper BER measurements (to characterize the margin), longer measurement times are needed.

The post-FEC BER is measured by running the FEC decoder on the received data and counting any uncorrected errors after FEC. For a target post-FEC BER of 1e-15, the measurement would need to run for 1e15 / (56e9 * 2) = approximately 2.5 hours at 112 Gbps to observe even one error on average. In practice, the post-FEC BER is estimated from the pre-FEC BER using the FEC code's known error correction characteristic.

### Q8. What are eye diagram measurements for compliance testing and how are they performed?

**Answer:**

Eye diagram measurements for compliance testing use specific procedures defined by the relevant standard. For IEEE 802.3ck (100G Ethernet), the measurement uses a high-bandwidth oscilloscope (at least 50 GHz analog bandwidth) with the signal captured at the compliance test point.

The measurement procedure includes applying a PRBS31Q pattern (for PAM4) at the transmitter output, capturing at least 10000 UI of waveform data, equalizing the captured waveform using a reference CTLE (to account for the test fixture channel), and computing the eye diagram from the equalized data.

The compliance metrics extracted from the eye diagram include the eye height at the specified BER (measured from the vertical histogram extrapolation), the eye width at the specified BER (measured from the horizontal histogram extrapolation), the TDECQ (Transmitter and Dispersion Eye Closure Quaternary), which is the power penalty due to transmitter impairments measured against a reference equalizer, and the transmitter SNDR (signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio) measured from the four-level voltage histogram.

TDECQ is a particularly important metric for PAM4 compliance because it captures the combined effect of all transmitter impairments (noise, non-linearity, bandwidth limitation, jitter) in a single number. TDECQ is defined as the ratio of the ideal eye opening (without impairments) to the actual eye opening (with impairments), expressed in dB. The specification typically requires TDECQ less than 3.5-4.0 dB.

### Q9. How is jitter measured and decomposed for SerDes compliance?

**Answer:**

Jitter measurement and decomposition separate the total jitter (TJ) into its constituent components (RJ, DJ, DDJ, DCD, PJ, BUJ) to enable root-cause analysis and to verify compliance with component-level jitter specifications.

The measurement uses either a real-time oscilloscope (which captures the full waveform and computes jitter from the edge timing) or a BERT with jitter analysis capability (which measures the BER as a function of sampling phase, the bathtub curve, and extracts jitter from the curve shape).

The decomposition algorithm first separates deterministic jitter (DJ) from random jitter (RJ) using the tail-fitting method: the tails of the jitter histogram follow a Gaussian distribution (from RJ), and the deviation from Gaussian in the center reveals the DJ distribution. The RJ sigma is extracted from the tail fit, and the DJ is the remainder.

The DJ is further decomposed into data-dependent jitter (DDJ, which depends on the data pattern and is measured by comparing the transition times for different bit sequences), duty cycle distortion (DCD, measured as the average difference between positive and negative half-period durations), periodic jitter (PJ, measured from the spectral analysis of the jitter waveform, appearing as discrete frequency components), and bounded uncorrelated jitter (BUJ, the remaining DJ after subtracting DDJ, DCD, and PJ).

The total jitter at a target BER is reconstructed from the components using the dual-Dirac model:

```
TJ(BER) = DJ_pp + 2 * Q(BER) * sigma_RJ
```

Where Q(BER) is the quantile function for the target BER (e.g., Q(1e-12) = 7.03).

### Q10. What is the role of channel simulation in compliance testing?

**Answer:**

Channel simulation complements physical measurement in compliance testing by predicting the link performance from the measured S-parameters of the channel and the specified transmitter/receiver characteristics. The primary simulation methodology is COM (Channel Operating Margin), which computes a single figure of merit (in dB) that predicts whether the link will meet the BER target.

COM simulation uses measured S-parameters as input (from VNA measurement of the actual channel), standardized transmitter and receiver models (specified by the standard, including TX FIR range, CTLE settings, DFE taps, and noise parameters), and optimization of the equalization settings to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio.

The COM result is compared against the specification threshold (typically COM >= 3 dB). If COM passes, the channel is expected to support the data rate with the specified BER. If COM fails, the channel must be redesigned (shorter traces, better material, fewer connectors, improved via design).

COM simulation is valuable for design-phase verification (predicting performance before hardware is available), for root-cause analysis (identifying which channel segment contributes the most loss or impairment), and for statistical analysis (Monte Carlo variation of channel parameters to predict manufacturing yield).

### Q11. How is signal integrity debug performed when a high-speed serial link fails?

**Answer:**

Signal integrity debug follows a systematic process to identify the root cause of a failing link. The process begins with symptom characterization: determine whether the failure is a complete link-down (no communication at all), an intermittent link failure (occasional link retraining or errors), or a marginal link (functional but with elevated error rate). Each symptom category points to different potential root causes.

For complete link-down, the debug process checks the basic connectivity (are the differential pairs correctly routed, with no opens or shorts), the transmitter output (is the driver producing a signal with the correct amplitude and frequency), and the receiver detection (can the receiver detect the transmitter's signal). TDR is useful for identifying opens, shorts, and severe impedance discontinuities.

For intermittent failures, lane margining is performed to measure the voltage and timing margins on each lane. Lanes with insufficient margin are identified as the source of the failures. The root cause of the reduced margin is investigated through S-parameter measurement (VNA), eye diagram analysis (oscilloscope), and comparison with simulation (COM analysis using the measured S-parameters).

For marginal links, the debug focuses on identifying the dominant impairment. The eye diagram reveals whether the limitation is voltage margin (ISI or noise) or timing margin (jitter). The DFE tap values (obtained from the receiver's register readout) reveal the ISI structure. The S-parameter measurement reveals whether the channel loss, return loss, or crosstalk is the dominant contributor.

### Q12. What testing infrastructure is needed for 224G serial link development?

**Answer:**

Testing 224G (112 GBd PAM4) serial links requires next-generation test infrastructure that can operate at 56 GHz Nyquist and beyond. The key instrument requirements include high-bandwidth oscilloscopes with at least 80 GHz analog bandwidth and 256+ GS/s sampling rate (currently available from Keysight, Tektronix, and LeCroy at the high end), 224G BERT instruments capable of generating and analyzing 112 GBd PAM4 patterns (under development, with early versions available in 2025-2026), VNA instruments with measurement capability to 110 GHz or higher for full-bandwidth channel characterization, and precision test fixtures with controlled impedance up to 70+ GHz.

The test fixture design at 224G is extremely challenging because any connector, adapter, or probe used to connect the DUT to the instrument introduces parasitic elements that can dominate the measurement at 56 GHz. Wafer-level probing (using micro-probes that contact the die directly) or precision RF connectors (1 mm or 0.8 mm connectors rated to 110+ GHz) are required.

On-die BIST (Built-In Self-Test) becomes increasingly important for 224G because external measurement access is limited. The on-die eye monitor, pattern generator, and error counter must be capable of characterizing the full performance of the 224G link, including eye height, eye width, and BER, without external instruments.

Simulation tools must also evolve for 224G: channel simulation must extend to 100+ GHz, equalization models must include non-linear effects (ISI, level-dependent bandwidth), and the COM methodology must be updated to capture the additional impairments that become significant at 112 GBd.
